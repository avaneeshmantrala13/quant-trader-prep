## Mastery & Calibration (`src/lib/mastery/**`, `src/lib/calibration/**`)

| Feature (code) | DOK entry + source | How it relates |
|---|---|---|
| Guessing-corrected Elo θ + per-tier difficulty `d`, `K=1/(1+0.05n)`, `d` frozen after 100 exposures (`elo.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: a chess-style skill rating per topic (θ) that climbs on correct answers and drops on misses, with a discount so lucky multiple-choice guesses don't inflate it; each difficulty tier has its own rating, θ moves fast at first then settles as you answer more, and a tier's difficulty locks in once it's well-measured._ | C3.1 — **Pelánek (2016) / Klinkenberg, Straatemeier & van der Maas — Math Garden Elo** | Directly implements the brainlift's "recommended spine": item-as-match Elo `P=1/k+(1−1/k)σ(θ−d)`, MC guessing correction, and the `a/(1+bn)` cold-start uncertainty knob. |
| Beta(α,β) posterior + credible interval, decay (`beta.ts`); verdict STRONG/WEAK/UNCERTAIN vs `MASTERY_BAR=0.8` (`verdict.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: a running estimate of your true accuracy on a topic that also tracks how sure it is, shown as a range; the verdict compares the low end of that range to an 80% bar and honestly reports STRONG, WEAK, or UNCERTAIN instead of forcing a yes/no._ | C3.1 — **Beta-Binomial Bayesian mastery** (author: **Johnson, Ott & Dogucu — _Bayes Rules!_ (2022)**; impl. **Dorai-Raj — R `binom`**) (+ threshold from **Bloom 1984**, C1.1) | The "how good, how sure" estimator judged against CI bounds not the point estimate; the 0.8 bar is Bloom's ~80% "cleared this node" mastery threshold. |
| Choice of Elo+Beta over neural KT<br>_In plain terms: deliberately using simple, transparent math instead of a neural network, because with little data per item the simple model does as well or better and you can see why it decided what it did._ | C5.1 — **Gervet (2020); Wilson (2016) "Back to the Basics"; Khajah (2016)** | Small-data regime (fresh items, sparse history) → logistic/Elo matches or beats DKT; Beta-Binomial (**Johnson, Ott & Dogucu — _Bayes Rules!_**) is the interpretable, uncertainty-carrying generalization of BKT (**Corbett & Anderson**, C3.1). |
| Misconception flags bumped on miss / ×0.5 decay on correct (`misconceptions.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: a per-topic tally of which specific wrong idea you keep using — a miss adds to that tag's count, and a later correct answer halves all the counts, so recent, still-active mistakes dominate._ | C2.3 — **Fischbein & Schnarch (1997); Konold (1993)** | Probabilistic misconceptions are stable and lecture-resistant, so the app tracks which specific misconception is live per topic rather than a single right/wrong score. |
| Reliability diagram, Brier gap, ECE-style `relGap` (`reliability.ts`); "when you say 80% you're right X%"<br>_In plain terms: a calibration check comparing how confident you claim to be against how often you're actually right — "when you say 80%, you're right X%" — where relGap is a single number for how far off your confidence is._ | C3.3 — **Dimitriadis, Gneiting & Jordan (2021) — CORP reliability diagrams / Brier decomposition** | Exactly the calibration machinery: predicted-vs-observed against the 45° diagonal, equal-frequency bins early (CORP/PAV later), Brier reliability gap. |
| Weakness ranking by CI_low; "confidently wrong" flag (`ranking.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: sorts your weak topics worst-first using the pessimistic end of the estimate so proven gaps beat noisy ones; "confidently wrong" flags topics where you feel sure but the calibration check says that confidence isn't earned._ | C3.3 — **CORP (Dimitriadis et al.)** + C3.1 Beta-Binomial | "Confidently wrong" = accuracy CI looks fine but the reliability diagram sits below the diagonal — flagged even when accuracy looks OK, exactly as the DOK 2 prescribes. |

## Tutoring (`src/lib/tutor/**`, `src/components/tutor/**`)

| Feature (code) | DOK entry + source | How it relates |
|---|---|---|
| Phase ladder worked → faded → independent (`phase.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: automatically moves you from studying a fully worked solution, to filling in progressively more blanks, to solving alone — driven by your skill rating._ | C2.1 — **Renkl & Atkinson (2003); Sweller & Cooper (1985)** | The concrete "complete example → increasingly incomplete → solve alone" fading spine; parametric generators make templating the worked solution and blanking steps nearly free. |
| Expertise-reversal re-scaffold on regression (`phase.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: if a strong learner starts missing, it snaps back to the full worked example; support is removed as you improve because too much hand-holding actually hurts stronger learners._ | C2.1 — **Kalyuga et al. (2001) — expertise reversal** | Worked-example support is faded/re-added based on the Elo/Beta mastery signal because scaffolding becomes redundant (even harmful) for stronger learners. |
| Faded steps blank the misconception-critical step first, then last-to-first (`faded.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: when it turns a worked solution into fill-in-the-blank practice, it first hides the one step people usually get wrong, then blanks the rest from the end backward._ | C2.1 — **Renkl & Atkinson** | Learners "learn most about the principles that were faded," so the step carrying the topic's core misconception is faded first. |
| 5-rung answer-withholding hint ladder (`hintLadder.ts`, `answerWithholding.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: five escalating hints that guide you to the answer without stating it until the last rung — name the trap, re-represent it, show a worked twin, run a simulation, then finally reveal — with an automatic checker that blocks any earlier hint that would leak the answer._ | C2.2 — **Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen (2015); Shute (2008)** | Elaborated, misconception-keyed feedback (d≈0.49) instead of near-zero right/wrong (KR, d≈0.05); the answer-withholding guard is Shute's "don't always reveal the answer." |
| Natural-frequency tree with final division blanked (`naturalFrequency.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: rewrites a probability puzzle as plain whole-people counts (e.g. "8 of 1000…"), which alone roughly triples correct Bayesian reasoning, and leaves the final division for you to do._ | C2.3 — **Gigerenzer & Hoffrage (1995) — frequency formats** | Bayes/conditional-probability items are rendered as whole-count trees, the ~3× reasoning swing (16%→46%) achievable purely by representation, zero LLM. |
| Elicit-then-confront sim — coin/dice simulation (`ConfrontSim.tsx`, hint rung 4)<br>_In plain terms: makes you commit to your gut answer, then runs a coin/dice simulation whose counts visibly contradict it — the reliable way to dislodge a stubborn intuition that lectures don't fix._ | C2.3 — **Fischbein & Schnarch; Konold; GAISE (ASA 2016)** | Forces commitment to the intuitive (wrong) answer, then confronts it with a simulation/count that makes the contradiction unavoidable — the evidence-based counter to lecture-resistant misconceptions. |
| Self-explanation MCQ from distractor rationales (`selfExplain.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: a quick "why is the right answer right?" multiple-choice question built from the item's own stored reasons, so explaining the step gets graded automatically with no AI._ | C2.1 — **Chi et al. (1989; 1994) — self-explanation effect** | A one-tap "why did this step work?" MCQ whose options are the generator's stored misconception rationales, exactly the cheap deterministic self-explanation hook. |
| "Explain in more detail" deep-dive, solver-grounded steps (`deepDive.ts`, `DeepDivePanel.tsx`)<br>_In plain terms: an on-demand deeper walk-through of the same example, built from the exact solver's own steps and pitfalls so its numbers can never drift from what the question tests._ | C2.2 — **VanLehn (2011)** + C2.1 **Chi** | Step-based interaction (d≈0.76, near-human) via decomposed solver steps, with optional self-explanation framing — moving from answer-based to step-based tutoring. |
| Fresh same-family sibling for tutoring (`regenerate.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: can spin up another problem of the exact same type with new numbers on demand, which is what makes worked-example-then-fade practice cheap and endlessly repeatable._ | C2.1 — **Renkl & Atkinson / Sweller** | A parametric generator emits the same problem type indefinitely, which is precisely what makes worked-example → fading cheap and repeatable. |

## Remediation (`src/lib/remediation/**`, `src/content/remediation/prereqDAG.ts`)

| Feature (code) | DOK entry + source | How it relates |
|---|---|---|
| First-miss retry-in-place before backtracking (`policy.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: one wrong answer doesn't send you backward — a little struggle is good, so it lets you retry/ease in place and only digs into prerequisites after repeated failure._ | C1.2 — **Kapur (2008; 2014) — productive failure** | A first wrong answer is not an instant backtrack; a short bout of struggle (retry/hint/easier tier) is pedagogically valuable, so only repeated failure descends the graph. |
| Bottom-out then descend a DAG edge; teach at the boundary (`policy.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: when you're truly stuck, it walks down a map of prerequisite skills until it finds one you can pass, then teaches the one step just above it — fixing the specific missing building block, not the whole topic._ | C1.1 — **Doignon & Falmagne — Knowledge Space Theory (ALEKS)** + **Bloom (1984)** | "Descend the precedence relation until a probe passes, then teach one edge up" is KST's outer-fringe stop rule; Bloom mandates remediating the *specific* missing prerequisite, not the whole topic. |
| Stopping depth — don't backtrack below what the student can already do (`policy.ts`, `config.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: it stops descending at the skill you can handle with a hint but not without — not easier, not harder — so remediation targets your actual edge._ | C1.1 — **Vygotsky (1978) — ZPD** | The right node is one clearable with a hint but not without; ZPD sets both the floor and ceiling of how far to descend. |
| Descent trigger tied to missing prerequisites<br>_In plain terms: a problem is only "bad-hard" when its prerequisites are missing; that missing-prerequisite signal is exactly what triggers dropping down to fix it._ | C1.2 — **Bjork & Bjork — desirable difficulties** | An item is an *undesirable* difficulty exactly when its prerequisites are absent — the signal to drop to the missing prerequisite. |
| ~85% probe tier (`probeTierFor`, `PROBE_P=0.85`)<br>_In plain terms: when checking a prerequisite it picks a difficulty you're predicted to get right ~85% of the time, so a real gap shows up as a miss while a fluke slip still passes._ | C1.2 / C3.2 — **Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019) — 85% Rule** | Probe at ~85% predicted success so a genuine gap surfaces as a miss while a slip passes, making the descend decision reliable. |
| Hand-authored 5-node prereq DAG + misconception→prereq edges (`prereqDAG.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: a small hand-built map of 5 core skills (arithmetic → meaning of probability → counting → conditioning/expected value) plus rules linking each misconception to the prerequisite it implicates._ | C1.1 — **Doignon & Falmagne (KST)** + C2.3 **Fischbein/Konold** | Adopts KST's structure/stance (hand-authored DAG + DFS, not heavy combinatorics); misconception tags map to the prerequisite they implicate. |
| SM-2 climb-back intervals `[1,3,7,16,35]`, interleave repaired + parent topic (`climbBack.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: after a fix, it re-tests on a spaced schedule (1, 3, 7, 16, 35 days) and alternates the repaired skill with the topic it feeds, so the repair sticks._ | C3.2 — **Settles & Meeder (2016) / SM-2 (Woźniak)** + C1.2 **Bjork** | Make the repair durable via SM-2 spacing and by interleaving the repaired node with the original (desirable difficulties). |
| `isNodeCleared` at CI_low ≥ 0.8 (`climbBack.ts`)<br>_In plain terms: counts a skill as repaired only when the pessimistic end of its accuracy estimate clears 80%, so it can't be declared fixed on a lucky streak._ | C1.1 — **Bloom** + C3.1 **Beta-Binomial** (**Johnson, Ott & Dogucu**) | Bloom's ~80% mastery bar applied to the conservative CI lower bound, so readiness is never inflated. |
